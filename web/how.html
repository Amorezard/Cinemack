How Cinemack finds your film

Cinemack is a language model that answers from a database of real film records instead of from memory. That's what lets it find a film from a vague description, and say plainly when it doesn't know.

Your question→Understand→Search ×3→Read→Answer→Check

What happens when you ask

Every question goes through the same steps. Switch on Details in the chat to watch them for any answer.

  1. Read the question understanding

    The model works out what you're asking: a film you named, a plot you're describing, or a recommendation. For a description it also writes a short guess at how a film database would summarise that plot.

  2. Search three ways hybrid retrieval

    It searches about 30,000 films by meaning (vector search), by exact words like names and places (keyword search), and by its guessed plot, then merges the three rankings into one.

  3. Read the candidates re-ranking

    For descriptions, the model reads the plots of the top 50 candidates and picks the ones that fit, taking a second look further down the list if nothing does. A year you mention counts as a hint, because people often misremember them.

  4. Answer from the records grounding

    The chosen films' records (year, director, cast, rating, plot) go to the model with instructions to use only those facts. If nothing matched, it says so instead of guessing.

  5. Check the answer fact check

    Every name, year, rating and film in the finished answer is checked against the records it was given, and the result is shown under the answer.

  6. You rate it feedback loop

    Thumbs up or down go into a log, and every thumbs-down becomes a candidate test question for the next round of improvements.

More than finding the film

Once it knows which film you mean, Cinemack looks up what the question needs, from sources it names in the answer.

  1. Facts and money TMDB, Wikipedia, Wikidata

    Budgets and box office from more than one source (shown side by side when they disagree, and in today's dollars), cast and crew, awards, release dates and US age ratings.

  2. What people thought reviews

    Rotten Tomatoes and Metacritic scores, Guardian reviews (the star rating and a one-line summary, with a link), which New York Times critic reviewed it (with a link; their text is never given to the model), and what audiences praised or criticised, counted by aspect with a quote for each.

  3. Critics and audiences side by side the Reception panel

    A film's details show critics and audiences on one 0–100 scale. Critics: Metacritic, or else the Rotten Tomatoes average rating (out of 10, times 10), or else its percentage. Audiences: the IMDb rating times 10, or else MovieLens (out of 5, times 20), or else TMDB. A gap of 15 points or more is called out. "Divided" means many very high and very low audience ratings; ratings that rose or fell later come from when MovieLens users rated it. A sudden burst of extreme low ratings (review bombing) and joke reviews are left out of the counts. Critic lines come from Wikipedia's Reception section, with the outlet each one cites.

  4. Why it did well or badly a statistical model

    A model trained on about 12,000 films with known budgets and grosses estimates what a film like this usually earns before release, and which factors moved that estimate. Answers call it an estimate and give its typical error.

  5. Links that fit the question trailers, reviews, where to watch

    Under each answer: the trailer (it plays only when you press play), the full reviews, box-office pages, or where to stream, rent or buy it in the US, depending on what you asked.

  6. Made for you accounts, settings, memory

    An optional account keeps your chats on any browser. Settings set the answer length and voice, spoilers, genres, years, running time and age rating for recommendations. Memory keeps short notes of what you tell it about yourself, and can connect a new question to an earlier chat. You can see, edit or delete all of it, or use a private chat.

How well it works

Measured on questions set aside for testing, including real "what's that movie?" posts from the TREC Tip-of-the-Tongue benchmark.

Strengths and limits

What to expect before you ask.

Good at

  • Finding a film from a half-remembered plot, even with the details slightly wrong
  • Directors, main cast, release year and TMDB rating for about 30,000 films
  • Saying "I don't have that film" instead of inventing one
  • Follow-ups like "who directed it?" or "what about the sequel?"
  • "Why did it flop?" and "what did critics say?": answers built from figures and reviews it can show you
  • Telling critics and audiences apart, and saying where they disagree

Not so good at

  • Very obscure films: the database keeps films with at least 50 ratings on TMDB, plus the most popular recent releases
  • Endings and twists: the stored plot summaries stop before the ending
  • Small roles: only the main cast members are stored
  • Box-office "why" questions: the model shows patterns across many films, not proven causes, and marketing budgets are not public
  • Speed: a long description can take 10–20 seconds, and questions are answered one at a time on a shared GPU
